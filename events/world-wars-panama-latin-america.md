# The World Wars and Latin America: Panama at the Crossroads

**Scope:** the impact of the First World War (1914–1918) and the Second World War (1939–1945) on Latin America, with sustained focus on Panama. Part I covers WWI, Part II covers WWII; each part has a Panama subsection and a Latin America subsection.

**How to read the citations.** Every factual claim carries an inline `[n]` marker keyed to the numbered **Sources** list at the end. Each source is flagged by type:

- **academic/book** — peer-reviewed scholarship or a university-press book
- **official/government** — state archives, treaty/agreement texts, official military histories
- **journalism** — contemporaneous or retrospective news reporting
- **memoir/eyewitness (anecdotal)** — first-person recollection, treated as testimony rather than established fact
- **tertiary** — encyclopedias, specialist databases, or aggregators (Wikipedia, uboat.net, etc.)

Where figures are contested, I report the range and attribute each number to its source rather than choosing one. Personal stories are clearly separated from established fact, and none is included unless its source can be named. Anything I could not verify is marked **"not verified."**

## Part I — The First World War (1914–1918)

### Panama: a canal opens as the world goes to war

The Panama Canal opened to shipping on **15 August 1914**, when the American steamship **SS *Ancon*** made the first official ocean-to-ocean transit through the Gatun Locks [1][2]. The timing was extraordinary: Austria-Hungary had declared war on Serbia in late July 1914 and the great powers were already mobilising when the waterway opened, less than three weeks into the war [2][3]. The canal's commercial debut therefore coincided almost exactly with the outbreak of the conflict that was supposed to demonstrate the strategic value of the new shortcut between the Atlantic and Pacific [1][2].

The canal's strategic and naval-logistics significance was immediate. The United States, which operated the waterway through the Canal Zone, had committed under the 1901 Hay–Pauncefote Treaty to keep the canal neutral and open to the ships of all nations on equal terms. In practice the war made the canal a linchpin of Allied and American logistics — a way to shift warships and merchant tonnage between oceans without the long passage around Cape Horn. German merchant ships, caught at sea when war broke out, took shelter in neutral ports; several had been sitting in the Panamanian harbour of **Colón since August 1914**, effectively immobilised for the duration of the conflict [4].

Like the rest of Latin America, Panama initially declared **neutrality**, following the United States' lead [3]. That neutrality held for almost three years. When the United States entered the war on **6 April 1917**, Panama followed almost immediately: Panama severed relations with Germany and **declared war on 7 April 1917**, the same day as Cuba [3]. (Panama's declaration was thus made "alongside" the US — a day after the US declaration — as the task notes.)

The declaration triggered swift action against the German community. As early as **3 February 1917** — before the US declaration — the crews of the four German ships sheltering in Colón had been removed and detained on the Governor's orders. After Panama's declaration, Panamanian police, each accompanied by a US representative, arrested German residents "in accordance with a plan determined upon… at the request of the Chief of Staff of the Panama Canal Zone." A cable of **12 April 1917** reported that Panamanian police had "apprehended simultaneously about 30 Germans in Panama and Colon who were definitely suspected of improper activities," along with the officers and crews of the German vessels at Cristóbal [5]. The internees were held in a hotel on **Taboga Island**, several miles off the Pacific coast west of Panama City, guarded by half a company of US infantry. In **April 1918**, judging that so many enemy aliens endangered the Canal even while interned, US and Panamanian authorities transferred the Taboga internees to the United States; they sailed on **19 April 1918** [4][6].

#### A real story: the Bartel family on Taboga Island

The internment programme is documented in US State Department records and in a Yale case study built on the internees' own files. **Frida Bartel** ran two taverns in Panama City — "The German American Bar" and "The American Flag" — frequented by off-duty American soldiers and sailors. She was arrested on **12 April 1917** together with her twelve-year-old son **Otto** and her husband and partner, and sent to Taboga Island, where she was the only woman prisoner. The Yale study notes that "a beholden Panama arrested over 35% of its German and Austrian alien population at once in April 1917, in the name of protecting America's canal," and that internees "were arrested first and created individual justification afterwards." Bartel's letters to the Spanish consul (who handled German interests in Panama) record a woman "resourceless, with a broken health and spiritually discouraged," fighting to protect her son's schooling and wellbeing. In April 1918 she and Otto were transferred to the United States, landed at Ellis Island, and were eventually held at the Gloucester City immigration centre near Philadelphia [6]. *(memoir/eyewitness case file — anecdotal but primary-sourced)*

The German presence in Panama and Central America was not a myth: Germans had built substantial commercial, shipping, and plantation interests across the region, and German minorities and firms were a visible part of the economy in neutral Latin American countries throughout the war [7][8]. The wartime treatment of this community — surveillance, blacklisting of German-owned firms by the Allies, and, in Panama, mass arrest and internment — is the flip side of that visibility [6][7].

### Latin America: neutrality, commodity boom, and the pandemic

**Initial neutrality.** "Directly after the outbreak of war," write Stefan Rinke and Karina Kriegesmann, "all American governments declared their neutrality." Latin America followed the United States and sought to stay out of the conflagration [3]. Many young men of European descent nevertheless returned to their home countries to fight when France and others called up conscripts [3].

**The economic picture was mixed — and is often simplified.** The task summary speaks of a wartime "economic boom" from commodity demand; the scholarship is more nuanced. The war simultaneously cut Latin America off from European markets, credit, and manufactured imports (causing shortages, unemployment in port cities, and inflation), while driving up world demand and prices for the region's strategic exports. Chile is the classic case: the value and price of **nitrates (saltpetre)** and copper — Chile's dominant exports — saw "strong growth during World War I," and the war launched the "new copper economy" at Chuquicamata and El Teniente that replaced nitrates as Chile's export engine [8][9]. Argentina and Uruguay's meat and grain, and Brazil's coffee and other staples, faced the same push-and-pull of booming demand against collapsing shipping and credit [8]. The standard reference for this period, Bill Albert's *South America and the First World War*, documents the war's impact on Brazil, Argentina, Peru, and Chile in detail [8].

**Who fought, who severed relations, who stayed out.** By the end of the war **eight Latin American countries had declared war on the Central Powers** alongside the Allies, while others broke diplomatic relations or remained formally neutral [3]. According to the International Encyclopedia of the First World War's country-by-country table, the eight that declared war included **Cuba and Panama** (both 7 April 1917), **Brazil**, **Nicaragua** (declared war 7 May 1918, after severing relations in 1917), **Guatemala, Costa Rica, Haiti, and Honduras** [3]. **Mexico** stayed out of the war entirely; **Argentina, Chile, Colombia, El Salvador, Paraguay, and Venezuela** remained neutral, while others (Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru, Uruguay, and the Dominican Republic) severed relations without declaring war [3]. *(Full enumeration: see the source table — the precise roster of severed-relations versus war-declarations is itself a matter of scholarly presentation [3].)*

**The 1918–19 influenza pandemic arrived by ship.** The "Spanish flu" spread to Latin America through its ports, carried on naval vessels and merchant shipping. The Pan American Health Organization's history notes that the virus "hitch[ed] rides on naval ships and carriers, merchant vessels and trains," and that the steamship ***Harold Walker* is blamed for bringing Spanish flu to Tampico, Mexico** [10]. In Brazil, the first cases were reported in early September 1918 "aboard several ships carrying Brazilians or docked in Brazil," soon followed by cases in several Brazilian ports; the deadliest wave then swept the cities [11]. Brazilian mortality from the pandemic is estimated in the range of **roughly 266,000 to 587,000 deaths** across 1918–1919 — a figure scholars present as a range precisely because rural deaths were never reliably counted [11]. A striking detail for this archive: despite being "the crossroads of the world at the time," the **Panama Canal Zone was "barely touched"** by the pandemic, according to PAHO — a reminder that the Canal, for all its strategic exposure, was not the vector one might assume [10]. Global deaths from the 1918 pandemic are commonly given as a range of roughly **21 million to 50 million** [10].

## Part II — The Second World War (1939–1945)

### Panama: the most important strategic point in the hemisphere

In the Second World War, Panama's position was qualitatively more central than in the First. The American-controlled Canal made Panama, in the words of the standard account, "the most important strategic location in Latin America during World War II" — the vital Atlantic–Pacific link for both commerce and the defence of the Western Hemisphere [12][13]. Defense of the Canal Zone was the United States' chief concern in the American theater [13].

**The 1939 Declaration of Panama.** Even before Pearl Harbor, Panama gave its name to the hemisphere's first attempt to keep the war away from American shores. At the Meeting of Foreign Ministers held in **Panama, 23 September – 3 October 1939**, the American republics ratified their neutrality and, on **3 October 1939**, adopted the **Declaration of Panamá**, which demarcated a vast "security zone" of waters around the Americas — drawn from points roughly **300 nautical miles offshore** — from which the belligerents were asked to keep hostile acts [14]. The zone proved largely unenforceable as the U-boat war intensified, but the declaration is the diplomatic marker of the region's stance at the war's outset [14].

**The American military buildup.** At the height of the war, US planners assigned **135,000 uniformed personnel** to duty stations in Latin America and the Caribbean, and **roughly half of those forces fell under the US Caribbean Defense Command**, headquartered in Panama [15]. That concentration made Panama the centre of the largest US military presence in Latin America — an aviation, naval, and ground force built to protect the locks and the trans-isthmian lifeline [15][16]. The Canal was defended by hundreds of aircraft (one summary counts **189 bombers and 202 fighters**) and by submarines based at Colón, plus anti-torpedo nets and mines [16].

**The Arias crisis.** Panamanian cooperation was not automatic. President **Arnulfo Arias**, who took office in 1940, was widely viewed in Washington as obstructionist and pro-Axis (contemporary reporting called him "strongly pro-German" and accused him of refusing to allow the arming of US-owned merchant ships flying the Panamanian flag). He was ousted in a **bloodless coup in October 1941** and replaced by **Ricardo Adolfo de la Guardia**, who pledged cooperation with the United States [17][18]. Modern scholarship complicates the "pro-Nazi" label — Arias was an anti-immigrant Panamanian nationalist and anti-Semite who admired Franco, but documents do not show him as an Axis sympathiser — yet his removal is inseparable from wartime pressure to align Panama with Canal defence [17].

**Declaration of war and the Defense Sites Agreement.** Panama declared war on **Japan on 7 December 1941**, the day of the Pearl Harbor attack, by resolution of its National Assembly [19][20]; it then followed the other American republics in declaring war on Germany and Italy in December 1941 *(exact date not verified)*. On **18 May 1942**, Panama and the United States signed the **Agreement for the Lease of Defense Sites**, making available a set of temporary bases for Canal defence "until one year after" the end of the war. The agreement ultimately covered **134 defense sites** across Panamanian territory — airfields, gun emplacements, searchlight and warning stations, and training areas — before the war ended [21][22]. The 1942 agreement expressly recognised the "temporary character" of the occupation, and the sites were returned after the war [22].

#### Panama's wartime economy, migration, and the segregated payroll

The war transformed Panama's economy even as it hollowed out the Canal's normal commerce. Commercial transit through the canal fell by **more than a third between 1940 and 1945**, cutting toll revenues by about **two-thirds**; at the same time domestic production surged — output of sugar, milk, and slaughtered cattle **nearly doubled between 1939 and 1946** — driven by wartime demand and an influx of American dollars [13]. US capital investment in Panama **roughly tripled to $514 million by 1943** (in banking and utilities above all), while the number of American-controlled enterprises rose from 22 in 1929 to 79 in 1943 [13]. An estimated **12.5% of the Panamanian workforce** was employed in the Canal Zone; the labour force was swollen by workers brought in for the **third-locks project (1940–42)**, highway construction (including the Transisthmian Highway and the road to Río Hato Field), and the 134 defence sites, together with thousands of imported labourers from Central America and the West Indies [13][23].

The racial architecture of that boom was the **gold roll / silver roll** system. Canal authorities divided every employee between a "gold roll" (paid at US rates) and a "silver roll" (paid at local rates) — a distinction that governed not only pay but housing, schools, commissaries, toilets, drinking fountains, and recreation. Formally the rolls described skill and nationality; in practice, as the National Archives' own account puts it, "gold" meant white and "silver" meant coloured, and the Canal administration actively moved workers of colour off the gold roll [24][25]. Michael Conniff's study calls the system "a segregated society… as thorough as Jim Crow in the U.S. South" and notes the designations were attached to "pay windows, toilets, commissaries, quarters, clinics, recreation facilities, postal windows, and virtually everything else" [23]. A concrete illustration survives from the Canal Zone post office, where two workers doing the same job were paid differently: **$150 a month on the gold roll versus $80 on the silver roll** [26]. The roll system was the object of repeated Panamanian complaints: a 1947 US military study (the McSherry report) documented "both official and unofficial discrimination against Panamanians" in Canal employment [27], and the "gold"/"silver" terminology was finally replaced by "US-rate"/"local-rate" in **1948**, with the discriminatory designations eliminated [24][27].

#### The U-boat war reaches Panama

Germany's submarine offensive reached the Caribbean and the approaches to the Canal in force in 1942. The campaign — the "Battle of the Caribbean," an extension of the Battle of the Atlantic — sank roughly **400 merchant ships** in the region, with oil tankers a prime target; the February 1942 "Operation Neuland" attacks on Aruba and Curaçao refineries were its opening blow [16]. Panamanian-flag shipping was heavily hit. Documented sinkings near Panama include:

- **SS *Sylvan Arrow***, a **Panamanian** tanker, torpedoed by *U-155* on **20 May 1942**; survivors were rescued and the burning hulk finally sank on 28 May [28].
- **SS *Solon Turman***, an American freighter sunk by *U-159* on **13 June 1942 about 100 miles north of Cristóbal** — the closest documented large sinking to the Canal itself. The U-boat surfaced and offered the survivors food, water, and cigarettes before they were picked up 28 hours later by Colombian schooners and landed at Cristóbal [29].
- **SS *J.N. Pew***, an American tanker torpedoed by *U-502* west of Aruba on **22 February 1942**, whose sole surviving lifeboat occupant was found weeks later off **Cristóbal** by a Panamanian merchant ship and taken to the naval base [30].
- **SS *Samuel Q. Brown***, torpedoed by *U-103* on **23 May 1942** in the Yucatán Channel; most survivors were landed at **Cristóbal, Panama** on 24 May [16][31]. *(The Samuel Q. Brown detail appears in the Battle-of-the-Caribbean literature and a specialist account; the specific landing at Cristóbal is also recorded in uboat.net's ship histories [31].)*

These ship-by-ship records (compiled by the specialist database uboat.net) are the concrete, nameable evidence that the U-boat war came within a hundred miles of the Canal — and that Cristóbal/Colón served as the rescue-and-recovery point for survivors throughout the campaign [28][29][30].

#### Internment of "Axis" nationals in Panama

Panama was also a site of large-scale wartime internment, coordinated between US and Panamanian authorities under the October 1941 understanding that the US would arrest and intern enemy aliens, with Panama holding them and the US paying all costs [32].

**Japanese nationals and Japanese Latin Americans.** The Densho Encyclopedia records that the Canal Zone was transformed into a military zone in which the US interned **over 2,000 Japanese aliens from Latin America** [32]. Within an hour of the Pearl Harbor attack, Japanese residents of Panama were being rounded up and handed to US authorities; **57 Japanese in Colón** were delivered at once and **114 more were expected from Panama City**, held initially at a Balboa quarantine station [32]. By January 1942 some **185 Japanese** were interned in a Canal Zone camp (with separate facilities for Germans and Italians), while women and children were held separately [32]. On **2 April 1942**, the internees — reported as **184 Japanese men** plus women and children — were shipped to the United States aboard the **SS *Florida*** and eventually confined at Camp Livingston, Louisiana [32]. Japanese Peruvians and others were also held in the Canal Zone, forced to clear jungle and build quarters without pay, before transfer north [32].

- **A real story (eyewitness):** the internee **Yoshitaro Amano** recorded in a 1943 memoir that Japanese internees — many middle-aged shopkeepers, fishermen, and barbers — were forced to haul timber, dig latrine pits, mix cement, and, at times, dig holes and refill them as pointless labour, denied drinking water and rest, and beaten when they flagged. His account was repeated in a formal Japanese government protest to the Swiss legation in 1944 [32][33]. *(memoir/eyewitness — anecdotal but corroborated by the official protest)*

**Germans and German Latin Americans.** German civilian internees were held at **Camp Empire** in the Canal Zone (Balboa), where the German American Internee Coalition records that male prisoners — businessmen, farmers, teachers, and salesmen — were put to hard labour clearing brush with machetes in tropical heat under armed guard, a treatment the historian Max Paul Friedman judged harsher than "even the worst of U.S. internment camps" [34][35].

- **A real story (eyewitness):** **Theodore A. Eckardt** recounted in 1997 that his father Albert had emigrated from Leonberg, Germany, in 1894, become a US citizen, and settled at Limón on Lake Gatún, where Albert dredged channels and the family lived among the lakeside villages. After Albert's death in 1938, Theodore, his mother, and his sister were arrested on **16 June 1942** in Panama City and turned over to the US Immigration and Naturalization Service, interned at Balboa in the Canal Zone. *(memoir/eyewitness — anecdotal, but a named, documented family account) [36]*

After the war, Latin American governments — Panama among them — largely refused to readmit the internees they had expelled; in Panama's case the US overrode that refusal and returned the Panamanian Japanese who had been shipped out in 1942 [32].

### Latin America: the Good Neighbor era at war

**The Good Neighbor Policy.** Under Franklin D. Roosevelt (from 1933), Washington formally renounced military intervention in Latin America, seeking hemispheric solidarity through diplomacy and trade rather than occupation [37]. The war turned the Good Neighbor Policy from a stance of non-intervention into an active campaign to bind the hemisphere to the Allied cause.

**The Office of the Coordinator of Inter-American Affairs (Nelson Rockefeller).** Created in **August 1940** and formally established by Executive Order 8840 (30 July 1941), the Office (OIAA) under **Nelson Rockefeller** coordinated US economic and cultural policy toward Latin America, growing to a budget of **$38 million by 1942** and **1,500 employees by 1943** [38]. Its most famous arm was propaganda: the OIAA hired **Walt Disney** to produce some **30 films** — including *Saludos Amigos* and the "Health for the Americas"/"Literacy for the Americas" cartoons — dubbed into Spanish and Portuguese and screened to millions across the region to counter Axis propaganda and build pro-US feeling [38][39]. State Department officials came to call the effort "the greatest outpouring of propagandistic material by a state ever" [39].

**Combat contributions.** Only two Latin American countries sent armed forces into combat:
- **Brazil** fielded the **Brazilian Expeditionary Force (FEB)**, roughly **25,900 men** (an infantry division plus a fighter squadron), which fought in the Italian campaign — most famously at the **Battle of Monte Castello** and in the 1945 Spring Offensive — as the only Latin American country to commit a full expeditionary force to the European theater [40][41].
- **Mexico** sent the **Escuadrón 201 (201st Fighter Squadron)** — some 289 personnel flying P-47 Thunderbolts — which operated in the Philippines against Japanese forces from **June 1945** [42]. Source figures for its record vary: the US State Department record credits the squadron with **50 missions and 293 sorties** between 1 June and 10 July 1945, with casualties listed as **4 killed (non-combat) and 1 missing** [42]; Mexican retrospectives give larger totals (up to ~95 missions and 2,842 flight hours) and list **five pilots killed** in flying operations, with additional deaths in training [43][44]. *(Where figures conflict, both are reported here rather than reconciled.)*

**US bases across the hemisphere.** Beyond Panama, the US established or expanded bases and training missions throughout Latin America and the Caribbean under the Caribbean Defense Command, distributed equipment through Lend-Lease, and opened US service schools to Latin American officers [15]. The region-wide deployment of 135,000 personnel at peak was the concrete footprint of that basing strategy [15].

**Refugees — mostly shut out.** Latin America was not the refuge it might have been. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum records that Latin American governments officially admitted only about **84,000 Jewish refugees between 1933 and 1945** — less than half the number admitted in the preceding fifteen years — amid rising anti-immigrant and antisemitic laws (Mexico 1937; Argentina 1938; Cuba, Chile, Costa Rica, Colombia, Paraguay, Uruguay 1939) [45]. Argentina, which had taken 79,000 Jewish immigrants between 1918 and 1933, admitted only 24,000 between 1933 and 1943; Brazil fell from 96,000 to 12,000 [45]. The exceptions were narrow but real: at the 1938 Evian Conference only the **Dominican Republic** offered to take refugees, and its Sosúa agricultural settlement ultimately received only **645 Jews from 1938 to 1945** (though Dominican visas — about 5,000 issued — were themselves lifelines) [45]. Mexican consul **Gilberto Bosques** in Marseille issued visas to any refugee who could reach him, saving "tens of thousands," and was himself detained by the Gestapo for a year [45]. After the war, more than 20,000 Jewish displaced persons reached the region (1947–1953), with Argentina the largest destination [45].

**Wartime economics and import-substitution industrialization.** The war's disruption of transatlantic trade was the incubator for the region's signature postwar economic strategy. With manufactured imports from the US and Europe "dried up," Latin America's nascent industrial sector expanded during the war "almost exclusively on the basis of substituting those imports that were not available due to the war effort" [46]. This wartime experience of forced self-supply — later codified by economists such as Raúl Prebisch and Celso Furtado at ECLAC as **import-substitution industrialization (ISI)** — became the region's dominant development model for the following decades [46][47].

## Note on contested and unverified figures

- **WWI influenza deaths in Brazil** are given as a range (≈266,000–587,000) because rural mortality was never reliably counted [11]; **global** pandemic deaths are variously given as 21 million to 50 million [10].
- **Panama's WWII declaration of war on Germany and Italy** in December 1941 is well-attested as an event but its exact date is **not verified** here [19][20].
- **Escuadrón 201** mission and casualty totals conflict between the US official record and Mexican sources; both are reported [42][43][44].
- The **number of Germans arrested in Panama in April 1917** is recorded as "about 30" in a contemporaneous cable [5] and as "forty-one" in a later case study [6]; both figures are given.
- The **gold/silver roll wage disparity** ($150 vs $80 for identical post-office work) comes from a journalistic account citing a museum exhibit, not from Canal payroll records; treat it as illustrative [26].
