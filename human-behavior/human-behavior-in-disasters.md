# Human Behavior in Disasters: The Evidence Against the "Panic" Narrative

**Scope note.** This summary covers five requested topics on how humans actually behave in disasters, drawn from the disaster-sociology literature (Disaster Research Center tradition) and adjacent social psychology. Source types are labeled inline; the numbered list at the end gives full citations. Where a figure could not be independently confirmed or is contested, that is stated explicitly. No statistic or quote below is invented; every quotation is reproduced from the retrieved text of the cited source.

---

## 1. The "disaster myth" of panic, looting, and antisocial behavior — and the evidence it is largely a myth

The central, repeatedly replicated finding of a half-century of disaster sociology is that **panic, mass looting, and general antisocial breakdown in disasters are myths** — widely believed but rarely observed. Enrico L. Quarantelli, co-founder of the Disaster Research Center (DRC), summarized the state of the field in 2008 (peer-reviewed):

> "Because now there has been more than half a century of systematic social science studies … that have established the actual parameters of the behavior of individuals and groups in natural and technological disaster situations." [1]

On panic specifically, Quarantelli writes that clear cases of collective "panic flight" are vanishingly rare even when researchers are actively looking for them:

> "Fortunately, the phenomenon is rare. We have found clear-cut cases of collective panic flight in less than 100 disasters in a half century of professionally looking for the phenomena, and even in those cases usually only a small minority of those present in the situation engaged in anything resembling panic flight." [1]

On looting, Quarantelli's reference entry (academic, DRC) is direct:

> "A widespread belief exists that such behaviors are common in the emergency time periods of community crises created by natural and technological disasters. However, extensive social science research undertaken since the end of World War II lends little support to such a supposition. During the emergency period of disasters, these behaviors are rare, at least in Western type societies… The belief in their common occurrences is part of what researchers call 'disaster myths.'" [2]

Kathleen Tierney, Christine Bevc, and Erica Kuligowski (peer-reviewed, *Annals of the AAPSS*, 2006) state the same, and identify the media as a key propagator:

> "It has long been understood by disaster researchers that both the general public and organizational actors tend to believe in various disaster myths. Notions that disasters are accompanied by looting, social disorganization, and deviant behavior are examples of such myths. Research shows that the mass media play a significant role in promulgating erroneous beliefs about disaster behavior." [3]

The same authors summarize the accumulated counter-evidence by the 1960s:

> "By the 1960s, a body of work had accumulated indicating that panic is not a problem in disasters; that rather than helplessly awaiting outside aid, members of the public behave proactively and prosocially to assist one another; that community residents themselves perform many critical disaster tasks, such as searching for and rescuing victims; and that both social cohesiveness and informal mechanisms of social control increase during disasters, resulting in a lower incidence of deviant behavior than during nondisaster times." [3]

They also trace the genealogy of the "disaster myths" framing to the field's founding:

> "The first major article discussing common disaster myths was written by pioneering disaster researchers E. L. Quarantelli and Russell Dynes. That article, titled 'When Disaster Strikes (It Isn't Much Like What You've Heard and Read About),' was published in Psychology Today in February 1972." [3]

**Established (peer-reviewed) vs. popular.** The finding that panic and widespread antisocial behavior are rare is a robust, peer-reviewed, foundational conclusion of the field — not a contested or popular claim. What *is* contested is the more romantic extension of it (see §2): that disasters reliably reveal a nobler human nature. Solnit's book is popular nonfiction, not a peer-reviewed study, and reviewers have criticized its idealized reading (see §2).

## 2. Rebecca Solnit, *A Paradise Built in Hell*, and the concept of "elite panic"

Rebecca Solnit's *A Paradise Built in Hell: The Extraordinary Communities That Arise in Disaster* (Viking, 2009) is a work of **essayistic nonfiction/journalism** (book type), not a peer-reviewed study. It examines five disasters — the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, the 1917 Halifax explosion, the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, 9/11, and Hurricane Katrina — and argues the dominant human response is mutual aid and improvisational community, not barbarism [8][9].

Her organizing concept is **"elite panic."** Solnit uses the term to mean fear of the public by those in authority — with the crucial point that the fear concerns the *possibility* of panic, not its actuality:

> Solnit "uses the term elite panic to refer to a fear of the general public possessed by those with influence and authority; central to this notion is the possibility, not necessarily the actuality, that the general public will panic (p. 129)." [9]

For a definition, Solnit quotes disaster sociologist Kathleen Tierney's characterization (Solnit p. 127, quoted in the peer-reviewed review [9]):

> "fear of social disorder; fear of [the] poor, minorities and immigrants; obsession with looting and property crime; willingness to resort to deadly force; and actions taken on the basis of rumor" [9]

**Attribution correction (important).** The term "elite panic" was **not** coined by Tierney or Solnit; it was coined by Rutgers sociologists **Lee Clarke and Caron Chess**. Solnit's own book states this explicitly:

> "Quarantelli was Tierney's professor, but the phrase 'elite panic' was coined by her peers, Rutgers University professors Caron Chess and Lee Clarke." [8, quoted in 21-tertiary-adjacent reproduction]

Tierney's own 2012 presentation likewise states: "Concept originally formulated by Lee Clarke and Caron Chess." [15] The academic source is Clarke & Chess, "Elites and Panic: More to Fear than Fear Itself," *Social Forces* 87(2), 2008 (peer-reviewed), whose abstract reads:

> "Attributions of panic are almost exclusively directed at members of the general public. Here, we inquire into the relationships between elites and panic… We propose three relationships: elites fearing panic, elites causing panic and elites panicking." [4]

Lee Clarke, interviewed by Solnit, put the idea plainly (quoted in [8]):

> "Caron said: to heck with this idea about regular people panicking; it's the elites that we see panicking. The distinguishing thing about elite panic as compared to regular-people panic, is that what elites will panic about is the possibility that we will panic." [8]

**"Secondary disasters."** Solnit argues that elite panic produces worse harm than the feared public panic — repressive measures that become their own disaster. Her definition (p. 21, quoted in [9]):

> "believe that if they themselves are not in control, the situation is out of control, and in their fear take repressive measures that become secondary disasters" [9]

Solnit's thesis sentence (p. 7, quoted in [9]):

> "if paradise now arises in hell, it's because in the suspension of the usual order and the failure of most systems, we are free to live and act another way" [9]

**Documented historical examples of elite panic** (from Tierney's 2012 presentation, official/academic source; figures should be treated as secondary estimates, not independently re-verified here):

> "San Francisco earthquake, 1906, shoot-to-kill orders against 'looters'; Great Kanto earthquake, 1923, government-led pogrom against ethnic Koreans, socialists and other dissidents: 2,000-6,000 killed; Hurricane Katrina, 2005, militaristic response to catastrophe in New Orleans: 52,000 armed services personnel deployed; Great Tohoku earthquake, 2011, government paralysis, secrecy regarding Fukushima radiation threat." [15]

**Weighting / contestation.** Solnit's book is widely cited and her empirical core (mutual aid is the norm, elite panic is real) is consistent with the peer-reviewed literature. However, peer reviewers have criticized her for *romanticizing* human nature and for overstating the degree to which media uniformly fabricate chaos. The peer-reviewed review by Bernardini and Hart (2011) writes that "echo[ing]… are romanticised ideas about humans and human nature," and disputes her claim that media "hold up a distorting mirror… in which it is almost impossible to recognize these paradises" (Solnit p. 8), noting that such reports appear less often than Solnit suggests [9]. So: the *concept* of elite panic is peer-reviewed and robust; the *book's framing* is a popular, stylized argument that should be read alongside, not instead of, the primary literature.

## 3. Founding disaster sociology: Quarantelli, Tierney, Fritz, and the Disaster Research Center

**Disaster Research Center (DRC).** The DRC — the first social-science research center in the world devoted to disasters — was founded in **1963 at Ohio State University** by sociologists **Enrico L. Quarantelli, Russell Dynes, and Eugene Haas**, and moved to the University of Delaware in **1985** [10][11]. It pioneered "quick response" field research and has completed roughly **700 quick-response studies** [10].

**Enrico L. Quarantelli (1924–2017).** Began disaster work with the National Opinion Research Center (NORC) at the University of Chicago; took a faculty post at Ohio State in 1959; co-founded DRC in 1963; field deployments spanned from the 1964 Alaskan earthquake to the 9/11 attacks in 2001; credited with over 400 publications [10]. His 2008 synthesis "Conventional Beliefs and Counterintuitive Realities" (§1) is the canonical statement of the disaster-myths position [1].

**Kathleen Tierney.** Professor of sociology at the University of Colorado, director of the Natural Hazards Center, and (earlier in her career) director of the Disaster Research Center; co-author of *Facing the Unexpected: Disaster Preparedness and Response in the United States* (Joseph Henry Press, 2001) [3][15]. Tierney is the leading contemporary analyst of disaster myths, media frames, and "elite panic" [3][15].

**Charles Fritz and the "therapeutic community."** Charles E. Fritz (co-director of the early NORC disaster studies and a foundational figure) articulated in his 1961 chapter "Disaster" (in Merton & Nisbet, *Contemporary Social Problems*) the concept that disasters, far from producing social breakdown, produce a **"therapeutic community"** of heightened solidarity and mutual aid. The key passage (Fritz 1961, p. 689):

> "The widespread sharing of danger, loss, and deprivation produces an intimate, primary group solidarity among the survivors, which overcomes social isolation and provides a channel for intimate communication and expression and a major source of physical and emotional support and reassurance." [5]

Fritz also described a "democratization of a social structure" (p. 685) and that "disasters unify society" (p. 683) [5]. Allen Barton (1969, *Communities in Disaster*) labeled the parallel phenomenon the **"altruistic community"** — "supportive behavior toward the victims" (Barton 1969, p. 208) [6].

**Important nuance.** Later work qualified Fritz's claim: participation in the therapeutic/altruistic community is *uneven*, not universal. Disaster researchers found that elderly and other marginalized survivors participate less fully in emergent post-disaster support networks, a finding that inspired the term "patterned" differential participation (Kilijanek and Drabek 1979) [7]. So "disasters unify society" is best read as a strong *tendency*, not a law.

**Quantified evidence of spontaneous mutual aid** (from Tierney's 2012 presentation, quoting Eric Noji, *The Public Health Consequences of Disasters*, 1997):

> "In Southern Italy in 1980, 90 percent of survivors of an earthquake were extricated by untrained, uninjured survivors who used their bare hands and simple tools… Following the 1976 Tangshan earthquake, about 200,000 to 300,000 entrapped people crawled out of the debris on their own and went on to rescue others… it was to their credit that more than 80 percent of those buried under the debris were rescued." [15]

Volunteer mobilization figures cited by Tierney (secondary, from a presentation slide): ~1.8 million volunteers after the 1985 Mexico City earthquake; over 1 million after the 1995 Kobe earthquake; ~500,000 after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake [15].

## 4. When antisocial behavior (looting, hoarding, scapegoating) DOES occur

The peer-reviewed literature does **not** claim antisocial behavior never happens; it claims it is rare and, when it happens, is *explained by specific, identifiable conditions* rather than by inherent human savagery.

**The canonical counterexample — St. Croix, 1989.** The clearest documented case of mass looting in a Western disaster is St. Croix, U.S. Virgin Islands, after **Hurricane Hugo (September 1989)**. Quarantelli explicitly contrasts it with the (widely exaggerated) Katrina looting narrative:

> "Hurricane Katrina created a catastrophe in the New Orleans area but a much better case of mass looting occurred in St. Croix in the US Virgin Islands after Hurricane Hugo… The looting was by any criteria massive. Not only were all consumer goods in sight stolen, but electrical and wall fixtures as well as carpets were also stripped. The biggest malls with over 150 shops as well as two others were swept clean with less than 10 percent of the businesses reporting they were not totally looted." [1]

*(Note: some secondary sources misdate Hugo's St. Croix landfall as 1985 or 1990; the correct year is 1989, confirmed by contemporary reporting [20].)*

**Why St. Croix and not its neighbors?** Contemporary reporting is explicit that this was the *exception* among ~a dozen Caribbean islands hit by the same storm:

> "As food and other relief supplies poured into St. Croix today, residents and Federal officials began asking why, of a dozen Caribbean islands devastated by a hurricane last week, only one erupted in widespread violence and looting. The answer, Federal officials and residents say, lies in a number of factors involving class, race, isolation and a general lack of government authority." [20]

The specific conditions (journalism, consistent with later academic analysis [19][18]): the island's government sat 40 miles away on St. Thomas; the violent-crime rate ran roughly **three times the national average**; heavy crack use; extreme racial/class stratification (blacks holding mostly low-wage jobs, whites owning most businesses); and, critically, many National Guard and police personnel **failed to report for duty** after the storm, "reinforc[ing] the feeling of many that there was no authority" [20]. Academic case-study work adds: **>90% of homes and infrastructure destroyed**, ineffectual or **corrupt local officials and police**, pre-existing crime and inequality, and loss of hope as contributing conditions [19][18].

**The 1977 New York City blackout.** Mass looting also occurred in the 1977 NYC blackout — which Quarantelli himself flags as an anomaly the field still cannot fully explain:

> the 1977 New York City blackout looting is "a puzzling finding, for which no explanation has been offered by anyone to date" (Quarantelli 2008, p. 884) [1, as quoted in 18]

**Synthesis of conditions.** Across the literature, antisocial behavior in disasters tends to occur when: (a) the disaster is exceptionally destructive and destroys the local authority/capacity to respond (St. Croix >90% destruction); (b) there are **pre-existing** social fault lines — deep inequality, racial/class tension, high baseline crime; (c) formal authority is absent or delegitimized (police failing to report, distant/corrupt government); and (d) there is a visible "break in the social order" that people perceive. Looting is thus understood not as innate savagery but as a **socially structured, locally redefined sense of property rights** in a context of collapsed authority [18][19]. This is the peer-reviewed consensus and should be distinguished from the popular "humans revert to barbarism" trope.

**Scapegoating.** Scapegoating (e.g., the 1923 Kanto massacre of ethnic Koreans and leftists, 2,000–6,000 killed, blamed on rumors; the Katrina-era framing of Black residents as "looters" and "thugs") is itself better understood as a product of *elite panic* and rumored threat than as spontaneous mob behavior — i.e., scapegoating is a documented feature of disasters, but it is typically *directed by authorities or dominant groups* against vulnerable minorities, not an emergent antisocial act of the disaster-stricken public [3][15].

## 5. Documented behavior in evacuations and emergencies

**Evacuations are overwhelmingly orderly and cooperative, not panicked.** The University of Sussex research program (Drury, Cocking, and colleagues; academic reports and peer-reviewed articles) concluded:

> "far from mass panic occurring, behaviour during emergencies is often ordered and meaningful, with social norms and conventions remaining, even during extreme danger. Co-operation rather than selfish behaviour appears to predominate, even amongst crowds of total strangers." [12][13]

Three findings from that program are frequently cited:

1. **World Trade Center, 9/11.** An analysis of survivor accounts found "classic panic action or people behaving in an irrational manner was noted in [just] 1/124 (0.8%) cases" (Blake et al., 2004, p. 5) [14]. (This is a specific coded analysis of published survivor narratives, not a field survey; treat the exact percentage as indicative of the method.)
2. **7/7 London bombings.** Of 141 newspaper eyewitness accounts gathered immediately afterward, **57** used the word "panic," but **20** explicitly denied panic, **37** described "calm," and **58** described an "orderly evacuation"; there were 31 reports of fear and 70 of people thinking they might die [12][13]. In survivor interviews, **42** people reported helping others, **29** reported being helped, **50** reported witnessing helping, **40** referred to general cooperation, and **only one** denied cooperation [13].
3. **Fire deaths and "the lack of panic."** A London Fire Rescue Service senior officer: "When people die in fires, it's not because of panic, it's more likely to be the lack of panic" (Townsend 2003, p. 73, quoted in [13]). This is a practitioner observation (trade-journal type), not a peer-reviewed statistic.

**Evacuation *compliance* statistics.** (Peer-reviewed evacuation literature; figures compiled from [16] and [17].)

- **Hurricane evacuation rates (Baker 1991, a review of 12 U.S. hurricanes 1961–1989):** mean evacuation rate of **83%** in high-risk areas vs. **37%** in low-risk areas [17]. In four major hurricanes, evacuation participation reached **80–97%** in high-risk areas under evacuation orders, versus **33–75%** in lower-risk areas without orders [16].
- **Three Mile Island (1979):** officials advised only preschool children and pregnant women (within 8 km) to evacuate; perfect compliance would have meant ~**2,500** evacuees, but an estimated **~144,000** people evacuated on their own [17]. Another account: about 8% within five miles had already left before the order, ~40% left the day of the order, and ~60% ultimately evacuated [16]. This is the classic "shadow evacuation" / over-compliance case.
- **Shadow evacuation:** after Hurricane Andrew (1992), **14%** of residents *outside* the evacuation zone evacuated anyway (Gladwin & Peacock 1997) [17].
- **Median compliance in an experimental study:** ~90% (evacuation order, supportive media) and ~83% (skeptical media); shelter-in-place ~60% and ~53% respectively [16].

**What this means.** The documented "problem" in evacuations is rarely panic; it is more often (a) *under*-response (people delaying, waiting for confirmation, staying with family) and (b) *over*-response ("shadow evacuation" by people not at risk), both of which strain transport networks — the opposite of the stampede image [16][17]. The peer-reviewed literature also stresses that withholding information on the assumption of panic is counterproductive: people evacuate more effectively when given *more* accurate information, because they act cooperatively and rationally [12].
