## 1. The Minnesota Starvation Experiment (Ancel Keys, 1944–45)

### 1.1 Design

The experiment was run at the University of Minnesota's Laboratory of Physiological Hygiene under Ancel Keys, beginning 19 November 1944, and published as the 1,385-page, two-volume monograph *The Biology of Human Starvation* in 1950 [1][3]. It had three phases: a 12-week **control/standardization period** (≈3,200 kcal/day), a **24-week semi-starvation period** (beginning 12 February 1945), and a **12-week controlled rehabilitation period** plus longer follow-up [1][3].

**Subjects:** 36 healthy young men, all conscientious objectors recruited from the Civilian Public Service (many from the Historic Peace Churches — Mennonite, Brethren, Quaker), volunteered to be starved to inform post-war refugee relief. **36 were selected; 32 completed the study** (four were dropped after breaking the diet) [1][3][4][5]. The original primary papers and the 1952 *Nature* review state "thirty-six were selected and, of these, thirty-two stayed the course" [4].

**The semi-starvation diet.** The primary peer-reviewed paper by Franklin, Schiele, Brožek and Keys (1948) states the caloric intake was "reduced ... from about 3,500 Cal during the control period to the average of **1,570 Cal for 24 weeks of semistarvation**" [2]. The 1952 *Nature* review gives the same figure — "an average of 1,570 calories a day" [4]. The diet reflected war-torn European rations: potatoes, turnips, rutabagas, dark bread and macaroni [3]. The design target was loss of ~25% of body weight over six months; rations were adjusted per subject to hold each man on his weight-loss curve [2][3]. Average body weight fell from 69.4 kg (end of control) to 52.6 kg (end of semi-starvation), a loss of roughly one-quarter of initial body weight [2].

> **Noted discrepancy:** the retrospective 2005 account by Kalm & Semba describes the semi-starvation diet as ≈1,800 kcal/day [3], whereas the primary 1948 paper and the 1952 review both report ≈1,570 kcal/day [2][4]. The lower figure is the one given in the original scientific reports and is the figure most commonly cited. Both figures are reported here; the difference is not resolved in the sources consulted.

### 1.2 Documented psychological and behavioral findings

The investigators coined the term **"semi-starvation neurosis"** for the syndrome they observed: "depression, narrowing of interests, social introversion, preoccupation with thoughts of food, decrease in spontaneous activity, physical as well as mental, and marked diminution of libido" [2].

**Obsessive preoccupation with food.** Food became "the one central and only thing really in one's life," as participant Harold Blickenstaff recalled [3]. The primary papers recorded that food was "the principal topic of conversation, reading, and daydreams for almost all of the Minnesota subjects," that cookbooks and menus "became intensely interesting," and that men "toyed with" their food, diluted it with water to make it seem larger, and dawdled over meals for up to two hours to prolong the pleasure [1][2][3]. 19 of 34 men read cookbooks; several collected recipes and kitchen utensils, and some altered career plans toward cooking or food production [1][12]. Carlyle Frederick reported owning nearly 100 cookbooks by the experiment's end [3]. Some men derived "vivid vicarious pleasure" from watching others eat or smelling food; gum-chewing and cigarette-smoking rose markedly [1][12].

**Food dreams and daydreams.** Dream content was studied systematically (unannounced morning dream-questionnaires), and dreams were coded into "food dreams" versus "other dreams," sampled during the 8th, 12th and 20th weeks of starvation and the 7th week of rehabilitation [2]. Daydreams were "frequent and often passionately devoted to food-related topics," with diarists reporting musings that "sometimes would continue for hours" [2].

**Apathy and depression.** Apathy was the outstanding feature along with depression [1]. MMPI Depression-scale scores rose from 54.2 (control) to 73.9 after 24 weeks of starvation, and were still 65.7 after 12 weeks of rehabilitation [2]. Men neglected personal hygiene — "too apathetic to bother with shaving, brushing their teeth or combing their hair" [5] — and several had to drop university classes for lack of energy and concentration [3].

**Irritability and mood swings.** Subjects became "hyper-irritable," impatient, easily annoyed, and prone to angry outbursts, with humor turning sarcastic [1][5]. Participant Carlyle Frederick: "… noticing what's wrong with everybody else, even your best friend. Their idiosyncrasies became great big deals …" [3].

**Reduced libido.** Libido fell markedly [1][2]. Max Kampelman: "I can tell you, the sex drive disappeared. There was none." [3] One subject reported "no more sexual feeling than a sick oyster" [5].

**Social withdrawal.** Social introversion and reluctance to participate in group activities were documented; men became self-centered, lost interest in others, and withdrew [1][2][3]. Participant Marshall Sutton: "We became, in a sense, more introverted, and we had less energy." [3]

**Self-harm (one documented case).** In the third week of rehabilitation, subject Sam Legg (No. 20) severed three fingers of his left hand with an axe while ostensibly chopping wood. His intent remains ambiguous — Legg himself said, "I am not ready to say I did it on purpose. I am not ready to say I didn't" [7]. The staff psychologist recorded that Legg's mental state was in sharp decline and that he was despondent over continuing hunger; the investigators classified it as a severe degree of "semi-starvation neurosis" [7]. (A further, more clearly anecdotal item — that one subject wished to contract tuberculosis so he could be fed in hospital — circulates in secondary accounts; treat as anecdote, not documented finding [secondary accounts].)

### 1.3 Rehabilitation and lasting effects

Rehabilitation was, for many, "psychologically the hardest phase" [7]. Some men became *more* depressed and irritable in the first weeks of refeeding [1]. Initially the men did not feel relief from hunger; the lowest refeeding group received only ~2,200 kcal/day (≈400 kcal above semi-starvation), and Keys later raised the levels [3]. Men continued to be preoccupied with food for months — "By week 20 food was still a vital concern and centre of interest for 11 of the 27 men" [1]. Appetite was "insatiable" at high intakes; on unrestricted refeeding some ate ≈5,000+ kcal/day continuously [1][4]. Sexual interest was slow to return (still "far from normal" at 3 months; largely recovered by ~8 months) [1]. Weight regained to ~10% above baseline before settling toward pre-experiment weight [1]. The MMPI depression score remained elevated (65.7) at 12 weeks of rehabilitation, indicating that emotional recovery lagged physiological recovery [2].

## 2. Stages of starvation and severe acute malnutrition (SAM) — clinical literature

### 2.1 Classification and clinical syndromes

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) is defined anthropometrically: weight-for-height/length below −3 z-scores (WHZ), and/or mid-upper-arm circumference (MUAC) below 115 mm, and/or bilateral pitting oedema [8][10][11]. Two clinical presentations are distinguished: **marasmus** (severe non-oedematous wasting) and **kwashiorkor** (oedematous malnutrition, often with skin/hair changes) [9][11]. WHO treatment procedures are essentially the same for both [9].

### 2.2 Physiological adaptation and its behavioral correlates

In starvation the body down-regulates metabolism: reduced basal metabolic rate, hypothermia, and hypoglycaemia risk are hallmarks of the initial stabilisation phase [8][9][10]. Hypoleptinemia (sub-physiological levels of the hormone leptin) is identified in the peer-reviewed literature as a key endocrine marker and mediator of the psychological/behavioral adaptation to starvation [12]. Clinical signs of infection (e.g., fever) are often **absent** in SAM even when infection is present, so antibiotics are given presumptively [10].

### 2.3 The WHO "10 steps" — where behavior appears explicitly

WHO's inpatient management of SAM is organised into **10 steps across two phases — stabilisation (days 1–7) and rehabilitation (weeks 2–6)** [9][10]:

1. Treat/prevent hypoglycaemia · 2. Treat/prevent hypothermia · 3. Treat/prevent dehydration · 4. Correct electrolytes · 5. Treat/prevent infection · 6. Correct micronutrients · 7. Start cautious feeding (F-75) · 8. Achieve catch-up growth (F-100) · 9. Provide sensory stimulation and emotional support · 10. Prepare for follow-up [9][10].

**Behavioral content in the WHO guidance** (peer-reviewed clinical manual):

- **Apathy and unresponsiveness.** A child's "return of appetite" (passing the appetite test) is the explicit signal for transition from stabilisation to rehabilitation — appetite failure is treated as a clinical danger sign requiring inpatient care [10]. Apathy and reduced activity are part of the clinical picture of severe malnutrition [9][11].
- **Abnormal behaviour.** WHO instructs clinicians to check for "abnormal behaviour such as stereotyped movements (rocking), rumination (self-stimulation through regurgitation) and attention seeking," and to respond with "extra care, love and attention" [9][10].
- **Delayed mental and behavioural development.** Step 9 states: "In severe malnutrition there is delayed mental and behavioural development," addressed with "tender loving care," a stimulating environment, and structured play therapy (15–30 min/day) [9][10].
- **Cautious feeding rationale.** Initial feeding must be cautious because of "the child's fragile physiological state and reduced homeostatic capacity"; a "return of appetite, usually about one week after admission," marks readiness for rehabilitation [9].

### 2.4 The starvation sequence (summary)

The physiological sequence of unrelieved starvation runs from depletion of glycogen through fat mobilisation to protein/muscle catabolism, with progressive metabolic down-regulation; death typically follows infection, cardiac failure, or electrolyte derangement, and refeeding itself carries a risk of cardiac failure if intake is increased too rapidly ("refeeding syndrome") [9][11][12]. The behavioral/psychological correlate across the clinical literature is consistent with the Minnesota findings: apathy, withdrawal, irritability, and food-centered cognition [1][2][11][12].

## 3. Historical famines and sieges: Leningrad, 1941–44

### 3.1 Scale and context

The German siege of Leningrad lasted ~872 days (September 1941 – January 1944). Death toll estimates centre on roughly **one million civilians** perished, though figures vary [15][16]. Bread rations were cut repeatedly; by 20 November 1941 they had been reduced for most Leningraders to **150 grams per day**, roughly half of which was non-nutritive filler (sawdust, cellulose, malt and other surrogates), and "almost no other rations were provided" [13]. Mass starvation deaths began in late October 1941; the peak came in the "Hungry Winter" of 1941–42 [13].

### 3.2 Hunger-driven social behavior

The most detailed scholarly account is Sergey Yarov's *Leningrad 1941–42: Morality in a City under Siege* (2017), based on hundreds of diaries, letters, memoirs and interviews [13]. Documented patterns include:

- **Food as the organising preoccupation.** Bread became "everybody's preoccupation"; "The main measure of a person's charity … was their preparedness to share bread" [18]. People ate their tiny portions with ritual slowness, covering the slice with a hand and eating crumb by crumb to prolong satiety [13].
- **Apathy and "torpor."** Apathy was "the most evident" consequence of starvation: impaired movement, lethargy, and "torpor," with survivors lying for hours beside dead family members "in a state of complete torpor" [13]. Soviet physicians used the term **"dystrophia"** for the starvation syndrome [18].
- **Theft, fraud, looting — and hoarding.** "Fraud, theft, robbery and looting" became the order of the day [18]. At the same time, some starving people **hoarded** food and money from their own rations: one worker was found dead having saved 3,000 rubles and a kilogram of sugar; another 1,500 rubles; an employee of the Public Library who starved to death in December 1941 was found to have hoarded food at home [13].
- **The "school of callousness."** Leningrad, Yarov writes, became "a school of callousness," as habitual exposure to corpses and mass death eroded dignity, self-esteem and sensitivity [18].

### 3.3 Family units — breakdown *and* persistence

The literature documents **both** directions, and this is the most important nuance for a preparedness archive. Yarov's work shows family ethics under "siege logic": parents were forced to choose which child received more food, which was evacuated across the Lake Ladoga "Ice Road," or which was abandoned; there were "instances" of domestic violence and of mothers stealing from their children [18]. Some families kept a dead relative's body in the apartment in order to keep using their ration cards [13]. Yet alongside this disintegration, Yarov finds "immaculate compassion" toward children and concludes that "moral precepts" persisted — "Leningrad saved itself through redeeming actions great and small" [18]. Reviews of Yarov note the parallel to Primo Levi's "law": traditional moral standards "were respected only if they did not endanger life," but certain rules held firm — food earmarked for children was "sacrosanct," exploitation of privilege was condemned, and failure to share brought ostracism [18][19].

### 3.4 Extreme cases: cannibalism — what is actually documented

**Source status.** Cannibalism during the siege is *documented*, but almost entirely from NKVD (Soviet secret-police) arrest records that were **not published until 2004**; before that, most evidence was anecdotal [20][21]. The historian Anna Reid notes: "for most people at the time, cannibalism was a matter of second-hand horror stories rather than direct personal experience" [21, quoting Reid]. This caveat is essential: the *scale* is known from police archives, but the *social reality* was largely rumor.

**Documented figures (from NKVD records, via Bidlack & Lomagin):**

- First recorded use of human flesh as food: **13 December 1941** (nine cases) [21].
- By December 1942 the NKVD had arrested **2,105 people** for cannibalism, divided into two legal categories: *corpse-eating* (трупоедство, trupoyedstvo — eating the already dead) and *person-eating* (людоедство, lyudoyedstvo — killing to eat). The latter were usually shot; the former were sent to prison [20][21].
- Of ~300 arrested for cannibalism in April 1942, only **44 were murderers** (person-eaters); the overwhelming majority were corpse-eaters [21].
- Demographics of those arrested: **64% female, 44% unemployed, 90% illiterate or with only basic education, only 2% with prior criminal records**; disproportionately **women refugees without bread-ration rights** [13][20][21].
- The Soviet Criminal Code had no cannibalism statute; convictions were made under "special category banditry" (Article 59-3) [21].

**Scale varies by source.** Bidlack (cited in a Rice University thesis) reports "over 1,500 arrests" for cannibalism over the whole siege; Reid's police records indicate "around 2,000 people" arrested by June 1942 for cannibalism or corpse-eating; the NKVD total to December 1942 is 2,105 [20][21]. All sources agree on the key qualitative points: **cannibalism was a real but relatively rare phenomenon relative to the scale of mass starvation, and it was predominantly corpse-eating by the most marginalised (undocumented women refugees without rations), not organised predation** [13][18][21]. Murder for ration cards was far more common than cannibalism [21].

## 4. Cross-cutting note for the archive

- **Documented (primary/peer-reviewed):** the full Minnesota syndrome (food obsession, food dreams/daydreams, apathy, depression with measurable MMPI rise, irritability, libido loss, social withdrawal, one ambiguous self-harm case) [1][2][3][4]; WHO/clinical SAM findings on apathy, rocking, rumination, and appetite-as-clinical-sign [9][10][11]; Leningrad social behavior from Yarov's diary/memoir corpus [13][18] and NKVD cannibalism statistics [20][21].
- **Anecdote / lower weight:** the "wished to contract TB for hospital food" item; Todd Tucker's vivid reconstructed detail (a reviewer noted Tucker cannot have known such specifics as "neatly mown grass") [6, review]; pre-2004 oral accounts of Leningrad cannibalism.
- **Unresolved numeric discrepancy:** the Minnesota semi-starvation caloric intake (1,570 vs ~1,800 kcal/day) [2][4] vs [3]; and Leningrad death-toll and cannibalism-count ranges (≈1 million deaths; ~1,500–2,105 cannibalism arrests) [15][16][20][21].
