## 1. Siege of Leningrad (8 September 1941 – 27 January 1944)

**The basic facts.** The German and Finnish blockade of Leningrad lasted **872 days** (commonly rounded to "nearly 900 days") [5]. Historians estimate that **roughly 800,000 to 1 million civilians** died — the overwhelming majority from starvation in the first winter of 1941–42, in a city of over 3 million on the eve of the war [3][4][5]. One historian's synthesis puts the civilian dead at 800,000, about 40 percent of the prewar population [5]. Sarah Gruszka, who wrote a PhD thesis on Leningrad wartime diaries, states that "historians agree that nearly a million people, mostly civilians, died during the siege — mainly of hunger, in the first winter" [3].

**Documented behavior under starvation (primary sources).**
- **Rationing:** Bread rations collapsed to starvation level. During the winter of 1941–42, rations fell to as low as **125 grams of bread per day** for most residents; an adult worker's ration was set at 250 grams of rye bread, half that for children and the elderly [1][3]. The bread was frequently "made from ersatz substances like cellulose" [3].
- **Subsistence coping:** People ate pets, cosmetics, wallpaper paste, petroleum jelly, rats and pigeons; leather was boiled into soup. For warmth they burned furniture, wardrobes, and books [3][4].
- **The "Road of Life":** small, inadequate supplies came in across frozen Lake Ladoga; the Soviets evacuated roughly 500,000 civilians across this route in early 1942 [1][4].
- **Crime and cannibalism:** theft and murder for ration cards became common; **over 2,000 people were arrested for cannibalism** in the first half of 1942 [3][4].
- **The "dystrophy" death spiral:** many who reached the terminal stage of starvation simply "gave up trying to live"; dead bodies lay in the streets [3].

**Tanya Savicheva — the iconic survivor diary (primary document).** Tanya (Tatyana Nikolayevna) Savicheva, born 23 January 1930, was an 11-year-old schoolgirl who recorded the deaths of her family members, one by one, over four and a half months in a small notebook, each entry written under a letter heading. The diary is held/displayed in museum archives (e.g., Los Angeles Museum of the Holocaust) [2]. Its entries, as reproduced in translation [1]:

> "Zhenya died on December 28th at 12 noon, 1941."
> "Grandmother died on the 25th of January at 3 o'clock, 1942."
> "Leka died March 17th at 5 o'clock in the morning, 1942."
> "Uncle Vasya died on April 13th at 2 o'clock in the morning, 1942."
> "Uncle Lesha May 10th, at 4 o'clock in the afternoon, 1942."
> "Mama on May 13th at 7:30 in the morning, 1942."
> "The Savichevs are dead." / "Everyone is dead." / "Only Tanya is left."

(These are English translations of the Russian original; the diary's final three lines in Russian are "Савичевы умерли / Умерли все / Осталась одна Таня" [1].) Her elder sister Zhenya died first — weakened by factory work and blood donations at a time when rations had collapsed [1]. Tanya herself was evacuated in August 1942, one of 140 children taken to the Krasny Bor orphanage, and died of intestinal tuberculosis on **1 July 1944**, aged 14 [1]. The diary was discovered and published after the war by her sister Nina [3].

**What it demonstrates.** The Savicheva diary is the most compressed surviving record of what mass starvation does to a family unit: death is normalized to the point where it is logged like a weather observation, and the psychological turning point is visible in the shift from recording others' deaths ("Mama died") to recording one's own abandonment ("Only Tanya is left"). The academic work of Alexis Peri (who analyzed 125 unpublished siege diaries) adds the key corrective: even as the siege "deconstructed" residents' sense of reality, most diarists did not simply descend into amoral survivalism — social relationships and values largely held [5].

## 2. Siege of Sarajevo (5 April 1992 – 29 February 1996)

**The basic facts.** The siege of Sarajevo lasted **1,425 days** (3 years, 10 months, 3 weeks and 3 days) — roughly three times longer than the Battle of Stalingrad and more than a year longer than Leningrad, making it **the longest siege of a capital city in the history of modern warfare** [8][9]. It was conducted by Bosnian Serb forces (politically led by Radovan Karadžić, militarily by Ratko Mladić), initially with the Yugoslav People's Army, from the hills ringing the city [8][9]. **Approximately 11,541 people were killed** — 5,434 civilians and 6,107 defenders — including about 1,600 children, with roughly 56,000 wounded, per the record of the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia (ICTY) [9]. An average of ~329 shells fell per day, with peaks over 3,700 in a single day [9]. Both Karadžić and Mladić were later convicted of war crimes including the siege [9].

**Barbara Demick's *Logavina Street* — real residents' stories.** Demick, then a correspondent for the *Philadelphia Inquirer*, moved into Sarajevo during the siege and spent the better part of **two years** profiling the residents of one six-block stretch, Logavina Street, drinking coffee with them and recording how their lives unraveled under siege [6][7]. The resulting book — *Logavina Street: Life and Death in a Sarajevo Neighborhood* (1996), republished in the UK as *Besieged: Life Under Fire on a Sarajevo Street* (2012) — follows named, real individuals [6]. Among the people she chronicled: **Jovan Divjak**, a Serb born in Belgrade who became a general in the Bosnian army precisely because he rejected the ethnic nationalists [7]; several ethnic Serbs who stayed and fought with the Bosnian army to defend the multi-ethnic city [7]; and an elderly Serb widow whose two sons fought on the other side with the besieging militia — whom, Demick notes, the street nonetheless treated with surprising decency [7]. Demick documents the survival economy of the siege: residents lost food supply, electricity, water, and telephones, and lived under steady mortar and sniper fire [7].

**What it demonstrates.** Demick's account is journalism of the strongest kind — sustained first-hand reportage of named, checkable people — and it illustrates the central finding that under siege, a functioning multi-ethnic community could persist: neighbors shared scarce resources and did not automatically turn on the minority among them. The siege's documented survival strategies (collecting rainwater, burning furniture and books for heat, running between buildings to dodge snipers) match Leningrad's — but the psychological through-line in the Sarajevo record is *social cohesion across ethnic lines under deliberate ethnic targeting* [6][7][9].

## 3. Holocaust survivor accounts

### Primo Levi — *Survival in Auschwitz* and the "grey zone"

**The book and the man.** *Survival in Auschwitz* (original Italian *Se questo è un uomo*, "If This Is a Man," 1947) is Levi's first-hand account of his year in the Auschwitz–Monowitz labor camp, where the Italian-Jewish chemist was deported in 1944 [10][12]. Two conceptual tools from Levi are essential here, and it matters for accuracy that they come from *different* books: the **"drowned and the saved"** distinction is introduced in *If This Is a Man* (1947), while the fully developed **"grey zone"** concept is the centerpiece of his last book, *The Drowned and the Saved* (*I sommersi e i salvati*, 1986; Eng. 1989) [11][12][13].

**The "drowned and the saved" — who survived.** In Levi's account, the camp ran a "pitiless process of natural selection" in which "the struggle to survive is without respite, because everyone is desperately and ferociously alone" [12]. The "drowned" (*Muselmänner*) were those drained of vigor into hollow shells destined to die; the "saved" were those who made it out — and Levi insisted, against the comforting myth that survival rewarded the virtuous, that the moral calculus was inverted. In his later essay "Shame," Levi wrote that those who survived were generally **"the worst: the egotists, the violent, the insensitive, the collaborators of the 'gray zone', the informers"** — concluding, in one rendering, "Those who survived were the worst, that is to say, the fittest. The best all died" [12]. Levi explicitly included himself among the "saved" and thus under this moral cloud, describing the resulting survivor's guilt: "I felt innocent, to be sure, but herded among the saved and thus in permanent search of a justification" [12].

**The "grey zone."** Levi argued that the camp could not be reduced to "the two blocs of victims and persecutors" [13]. Between pure victim and pure perpetrator lay a vast, ambiguous middle — prisoner-functionaries (sweepers, block clerks, kapos, and the *Sonderkommandos* who ran the gas chambers and crematoria) whom the SS coerced into complicity in exchange for marginal privileges (an extra bowl of soup, postponement of death). The Nazis' purpose, Levi argued, was to "contaminate the victims with evil, to shift guilt and blame onto them" [12]. Crucially, Levi was *not* a relativist: he drew a hard line at equating victims with perpetrators, writing that "to confuse [the murderers] with their victims is a moral disease or an aesthetic affectation or a sinister sign of complicity; above all, it is a precious service rendered (intentionally or not) to the negators of truth" [13]. His point about judgment is subtler: ordinary prisoners were deprived "to a condition of sheer survival... in which the room for choices (especially moral choices) was reduced to nothing," so outsiders remote from starvation have no standing to judge them [12].

**What it demonstrates.** Levi is the most analytically rigorous witness to the fact that **extreme privation degrades ordinary morality**: hunger and coercion do not ennoble victims — they narrow choice until almost no "good" option remains, and the people who survive may be those most willing to compromise. This is the strongest documented counterweight to the romantic idea (popular in much survival literature) that hardship brings out the best in people.

### Viktor Frankl — *Man's Search for Meaning*

**The book and the man.** *Man's Search for Meaning* (original German *...trotzdem Ja zum Leben sagen*, 1946; first English translation 1959) is psychiatrist Viktor Frankl's account of three years in Nazi camps (Theresienstadt, then Auschwitz, and later other camps), in which his parents, brother, and pregnant wife all perished [14][15]. It is simultaneously a first-person memoir and the founding statement of **logotherapy** — the theory that the primary human drive is not pleasure (Freud) but the search for meaning [14][15].

**Who survived, in Frankl's account.** Frankl's central empirical claim from the camps is that survival depended less on physical robustness than on an **inner hold on meaning and a future**. He wrote that the prisoners who endured best were not always the physically toughest, and offered the "apparent paradox that some prisoners of a less hardy make-up often seemed to survive camp life better than did those of a robust nature" — explaining that "sensitive people" with a rich inner life "were able to retreat from their terrible surroundings to a life of inner riches and spiritual freedom" [15]. Conversely, those who lost a sense of future, or "let go of their moral and spiritual selves," quickly declined — in camp language, became *Muselmänner*, "prisoners who have lost the will to live" [14][15]. Frankl located the mechanism in an **inner decision**: "everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances" (paraphrased; the book's core thesis) [14][15].

**The will to meaning — his most cited passage.** On a pre-dawn forced march, Frankl clung to the image of his wife (who, unknown to him, had already died), and recorded:

> "A thought transfixed me: for the first time in my life I saw the truth as it is set into song by so many poets... The truth – that love is the ultimate and the highest goal to which man can aspire. Then I grasped the meaning of the greatest secret that human poetry and human thought and belief have to impart: **The salvation of man is through love and in love.**" [15]

He generalized this into the formulation widely associated with him: that a person who knows a "why" to live can bear almost any "how" — a paraphrase of the Nietzsche line Frankl repeatedly invokes (exact English wording varies by translation; not quoted verbatim here).

**What it demonstrates.** Frankl supplies the psychological complement to Levi's moral account: survival is not merely physical, but is strongly predicted by whether a person can locate *meaning* — a task to finish, a person to return to, or dignity in how one suffers. His three pathways to meaning (completing a task, loving another, facing unavoidable suffering with dignity) [14] are among the most-cited frameworks in the survival-psychology literature that follows (see §6).

## 4. The Rwandan genocide — Immaculée Ilibagiza, *Left to Tell*

**The account.** *Left to Tell: Discovering God Amidst the Rwandan Holocaust* (2006) is Ilibagiza's autobiographical account of surviving the 1994 Rwandan genocide, in which an estimated 800,000 to 1 million Tutsis were killed over roughly 100 days [16][18]. When the killing began, her father sent the 22-year-old university student to the home of a Hutu Lutheran pastor (Murinzi), who concealed her and **seven other women in a tiny bathroom measuring roughly 3 feet by 4 feet**, hidden behind a wardrobe in the pastor's bedroom [16][17][18]. They remained there in near-total silence for **91 days** — the women could not flush the toilet unless they heard another toilet flush in the house, and were fed only leftovers from the pastor's family plates, since the pastor was hiding them even from his own family [18]. At one point machete-wielding searchers came as close as the bathroom doorknob and turned away [18]. Most of her family — father Leonard, mother Rose, and brothers Damascene and Vianney — was killed; her brother Aimable, studying abroad in Senegal, survived [16][17]. By the time the wardrobe was moved aside on 16 July 1994, Ilibagiza had lost about half her body weight [18]. The book's second arc is her eventual, deliberate forgiveness of the killers (including a childhood friend who had hunted her), which she has spent decades recounting publicly [17][18].

**What it demonstrates.** *Left to Tell* is a documented first-person record of survival by *concealment and dependence on an individual protector* — the opposite pole from Leningrad/Sarajevo's communal endurance. Psychologically it illustrates two themes from the survival literature: (a) the survival value of a meaning-frame under prolonged, silent terror (Ilibagiza credits intense prayer and her Catholic faith as the mechanism that kept her from despair and discovery) [17][18]; and (b) the documented capacity for post-survival forgiveness as a coping strategy, rather than vengeance [17]. Note that this is a single, faith-framed eyewitness account — powerful but anecdotal, and best read alongside the broader genocide historiography rather than as a representative sample of survivor experience.

## 5. Selco Begovic — Bosnian war survival (FLAGGED: first-person anecdotal / prepper-community)

**⚠️ Source classification: this entry is first-person anecdotal testimony circulated within the survival/prepper community, NOT peer-reviewed or journalistically verified. Treat as lowest-evidence-weight. His story has not been independently fact-checked by journalists or historians, and some specifics (which city, exact dates) are deliberately vague in his own telling.**

**Who he is and what he claims.** "Selco" (Selco Begovic) runs the website *SHTFSchool.com*, which sells urban-survival courses and books, and posts first-person articles about surviving the Bosnian war (1992–95). His central claim, stated on his own site: for **one year** he "lived and survived in a city WITHOUT: electricity, fuel, running water, real food distribution, or distribution of any goods, or any kind of organized law or government," describing it as a real "SHTF" (shit-hits-the-fan) situation [19]. His *The SHTF Anthology* (2018) repeats: "I found myself in one of those sieged cities. I lived like that for a year and I survived" [19]. He describes a collapse of institutions — no organized police or army ("anybody who had a gun, fight for his own house and his own family"), gangs operating after one or two months, the hospital "looked like butchery," "80 percent of hospital staff gone home" [19]. He credits his survival to a large extended family (15 members in one house), a few firearms (5–6 pistols, 3 Kalashnikovs), and especially his medical knowledge as a paramedic, which remained valuable even with no medication [19]. He reports his account began as a 2011 online forum thread that "is long gone," and that his writing now lives on his own blog [19].

**Accuracy caveats (required by the citation standard).** Selco's own material does *not* clearly name Sarajevo; he repeatedly says "a city" / "one of those sieged cities" in the Balkan region [19]. He is frequently marketed and discussed in prepper circles as "the guy who survived the siege of Sarajevo," but his self-description is vaguer than that. His experience (a year in a blockaded Bosnian town) is broadly consistent with the documented sieges of the Bosnian war — several Bosnian towns besides Sarajevo were besieged for months to years [19] — but his specific account is **not verified** against independent historical sources. His practical survival claims (trade and barter, family firearms, medical skill as a survival asset, the speed with which institutions and social trust collapse) are plausible and consistent with documented siege accounts, but as evidence they are anecdote, not data.

**What it demonstrates (if taken as anecdote).** As a *narrative* of the survivor's mindset, Selco's account illustrates several recurring themes in the psychology literature: (1) prior skill/preparation (his medical training) provided both practical value and a sense of purpose under collapse; (2) social bonds (the large family unit) were a concrete survival resource; and (3) the rapid degradation of trust and institutions under prolonged deprivation matches the documented pattern in Leningrad and Sarajevo. These points are interesting and consistent with stronger sources — but they should be attributed to the stronger sources, not to Selco, when the archive needs hard evidence.

## 6. The psychology literature: who survives, and why

### John Leach — the "10–80–10" rule

**The claim.** John Leach, a psychologist specializing in human behavior in emergencies, proposed in *Survival Psychology* (1994) that in a life-threatening crisis the population divides roughly into three groups: about **10%** keep their heads, assess the situation, and act rationally and effectively; about **80%** are "stunned and bewildered" — reasoning impaired, behavior reflexive and mechanical, effectively frozen or passive in the first moments; and the final **10%** become counterproductive — hysterical, panicking, or acting in ways that endanger themselves and others [20][21]. Leach's own research (e.g., on parachutists and on the 1994 *MV Estonia* sinking, where 852 died and survivors described passengers standing still on staircases as water rose) grounds the claim that cognition itself degrades under threat — working memory narrows and access to long-term memory is disrupted precisely when it is needed [21]. Note: the exact percentages are rough and vary across studies (some put the middle band at 65–70%), but the overall shape — a small effective minority, a large stunned majority, a small harmful minority — is widely reproduced [21].

### Laurence Gonzales — *Deep Survival* (survivor mindset)

**The book.** *Deep Survival: Who Lives, Who Dies, and Why* (2003, W. W. Norton) analyzes hundreds of survival and disaster case studies to isolate what separates survivors from casualties [22]. Gonzales's headline finding, from search-and-rescue data, is that **survivors are not the best-equipped or toughest** — rescuers "would find someone dead who was in possession of all the equipment he needed to survive... and someone alive who... would be in possession of none" [23]. The recurring traits of survivors he identifies include: they gather information and prepare in advance (not "weekend warriors"); they are **persistent, organized, and able to calm themselves**, then formulate a step-by-step plan; and — counter to the lone-hero myth — they are **socially connected**, with a reason to live anchored in other people ("I have to get back to see my son") [23]. His operational antidote to the "brain lock" of panic is **breathe, organize, act** — controlled breathing to suppress the fight-or-flight cascade, then a plan of small, achievable steps [23]. He draws an explicit line to Frankl, noting that survival requires "acting on behalf of something bigger than self-interest," and cites Frankl's clinging to his wife's image as a model of the social/emotional bond that sustains life [23].

### Amanda Ripley — *The Unthinkable* (the disaster arc)

**The book.** *The Unthinkable: Who Survives When Disaster Strikes—and Why* (2008, Crown) synthesizes survivor interviews and disaster research (9/11, Hurricane Katrina, fires, stampedes, plane crashes) into what Ripley calls the **survival arc**, three phases people pass through [24][25]:

1. **Denial** — the near-universal first response is not panic but "cool lethargy": people move slowly, ignore alarms, collect belongings, or "mill about" talking to others, driven by **normalcy bias** ("this can't be happening to me") and a fear of social embarrassment from overreacting. Ripley: on 9/11 many stayed at their desks checking email rather than heading for the stairs [24][25].
2. **Deliberation** — denial breaks and "we know something is terribly wrong, but we don't know what to do about it." Fear impairs cognition: tunnel vision, distorted time sense, degraded fine motor control. People "mill" — checking with others (on average, four or five sources before evacuating a hurricane) [25].
3. **The decisive moment** — people finally act, or they **shut down** (paralysis is far more common than panic; true panic requires feeling trapped, helpless, and isolated). Those with prior training/plans, high self-confidence ("I shape my own destiny"), and a sense of duty to others are most likely to act effectively [25].

Ripley's practical conclusion is that the single most valuable thing in a disaster is **information**, and that **training and rehearsal** — having already walked the escape route, having a plan — is what converts a would-be stunner into a decisive actor [24][25].

## 7. Synthesis: what the documented record says about who survives

Across primary accounts and the psychology literature, a consistent picture emerges, and it is more nuanced than either "survival of the fittest" or "survival of the virtuous":

1. **Physical robustness is secondary.** Frankl's "less hardy" prisoners, Gonzales's ill-equipped survivors, and Leningrad's resilient diarists all point the same way: prior fitness matters less than prior *mindset and preparation* [15][23][5].
2. **Meaning and social bonds predict survival.** Frankl's will-to-meaning, Gonzales's social connectedness, and Ripley's finding that those with "something to live for" act decisively are three independent corroborations of the same result [15][23][25]. Ilibagiza's faith and Frankl's wife-image are the two clearest first-person illustrations [17][15].
3. **Extreme privation degrades ordinary morality — and most people are not heroes or villains but the "stunned 80%."** Levi's grey zone and the drowned/saved inversion are the hard counterweight: under starvation and coercion, "the best all died" and survivors were often the most compromised [12]. Leach's 10–80–10 and Ripley's denial/paralysis show that *passivity, not panic*, is the modal human response to catastrophe [21][25].
4. **Community cohesion can survive even ethnic targeting.** Demick's Logavina Street is the key documented case that multi-ethnic neighbors protected each other through a 1,425-day siege — evidence against the assumption that collapse automatically means every-man-for-himself [6][7].
5. **The prepper narrative is anecdote, not evidence.** Selco Begovic's account is vivid and internally consistent with the documented record, but it is self-published, self-interested (he sells survival courses), and unverified — useful as a mindset illustration, never as a factual source [19].
