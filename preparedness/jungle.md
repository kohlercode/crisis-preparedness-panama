# Hunkering down in the Panamanian jungle / forest

Read this before you plan any "go into the forest" scenario. It covers the real hazards of Panama's tropical rainforest (Darién, Chiriquí highlands, the Caribbean coast) and how to stay alive if you are genuinely stranded there — and it opens with the honest reason this should almost never be your plan.

## 0. The honest framing (read this first)

- **Bugging out into wilderness is a last resort, not a plan.** In the large majority of scenarios, **sheltering in place is safer** than fleeing into the wild. "Heading to the woods" is not a destination.
- **Tropical forest specifically is a hard place to hunker down.** A dense canopy means **rescue is much less likely** than in open terrain — you will probably have to walk yourself out — and movement through secondary growth can be brutally slow (under 100 m/hour). On top of that you carry the burdens below: envenomation, insect-borne disease, wound infection, water, and the sheer heat/humidity.
- **The realistic posture:**
  1. Shelter in a hardened structure where possible (`home.md`).
  2. If forced to move, go to a **known occupied location** — a town, a road, a ranger post, a finca — *not* "into the forest."
  3. Only if genuinely stranded in forest should the skills below be used — and then the goal is to **stay put near a known route or river, stay visible, and get out quickly**, not to live off the land.

This is not generic prepper warning — it's the consensus of wilderness-survival and bug-out guidance, and it applies doubly to a rainforest.

## 1. The hazards (what can actually hurt you)

### Snakes — the terciopelo ("equis") is #1

- **Fer-de-lance / terciopelo (*Bothrops asper*)** — in Panama called **"equis"** for the X-shaped back markings. It is responsible for **more envenoming snakebites than any other species in its range**: abundant, terrestrial, **nocturnal**, and living near settlements, farms, stream banks, and trails. Venom is **hemotoxic/cytotoxic** — rapid local pain, swelling, tissue necrosis, bleeding/coagulation defects, which can progress to kidney injury and death or permanent disability.
- **Bushmaster / verrugosa (*Lachesis*)** — the longest venomous snake in the hemisphere (2–4 m); hemotoxic like the terciopelo but rarer and more reclusive. Unlike most pit vipers it **lays eggs and may defend the nest** — don't disturb leaf-litter mounds or hollow logs.
- **Coral snake (*Micrurus nigrocinctus*)** — **neurotoxic**: descending flaccid paralysis → respiratory failure. Rare but medically serious.
- **Eyelash viper / palm viper (*Bothriechis schlegelii*)** — small, **arboreal**, coils in palms and vines at **face height**. This is the snake you're most likely to meet by reaching into vegetation. No confirmed deaths, but bites are medically serious.
- **How to avoid bites (most are below the knee):** watch where you step and place hands, especially at night and near streams; use a light; don't reach blindly into leaf litter, logs, or dense vegetation; high boots + gaiters. **Do not try to kill or handle it — most bites happen during attempts to kill snakes.**

### Scorpions, spiders, ants

- **Scorpions (*Tityus*)** — medically significant; young children at highest risk.
- **Wandering/banana spiders (*Phoneutria*)** — neurotoxic, medically significant. **Recluse spiders (*Loxosceles*)** — necrotic bites. Tarantulas are painful but not life-threatening.
- **Bullet ant (*Paraponera clavata*)** — the most painful insect sting known. **Army ants / fire ants** — the real risk is multiple stings from standing on a column or nest.
- **Routine:** shake out boots, clothing, and bedding before use; don't sleep directly on the ground; use a stick to part vegetation, not your hands.

### Insect-borne disease — the bigger, quieter killer

| Disease | Risk in Panama | Key prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Dengue** | **Highest** — year-round; record **32,077 cases / 52 deaths in 2024**. Day-biting *Aedes* | repellent, long sleeves/pants, remove standing water |
| **Malaria** | Regional only: **Bocas del Toro, Chiriquí, Colón, Darién (incl. Emberá), Guna Yala, Ngäbe-Buglé, eastern Panamá, Veraguas**. **None** in Panama City / Canal Zone. Mostly *P. vivax* (97%) | night-biting *Anopheles*; net + repellent at night; prophylaxis for Darién/Guna Yala/east |
| **Leishmaniasis** | **~3,000 cases/yr**, *L. panamensis*; sandfly-borne (dusk–dawn), **sandflies pass through ordinary netting** | **fine-mesh net**, sleep elevated, repellent, covered skin |
| Chikungunya / Zika | Present | same *Aedes* avoidance |

- **The bite-prevention bundle:** EPA-registered repellent (DEET/picaridin), **permethrin on clothing/shoes/nets**, long sleeves + pants, a **fine-mesh bed net**, sleep elevated and screened.

### Wound infection

In tropical heat and humidity, **even a small scratch can quickly become seriously infected**. Clean and cover every cut. This is a major, underrated hazard of the jungle.

## 2. Shelter — the standard tropical system

The ground is where the hazards concentrate: snakes, scorpions, ants, spiders, flooding, and sandflies. **Sleep off the ground.**

**The standard system:** an **elevated hammock** with an **integrated fine-mesh bug net**, a **tarp pitched over it (ridgeline)** for rain, and **permethrin** on the net and hammock.

- **Fine mesh matters** — ordinary mosquito netting lets leishmaniasis-carrying sandflies through. Get "no-see-um"/sandfly-grade mesh.
- At higher elevation (Chiriquí highlands) or cool nights, you lose heat from below — add an **under-quilt or pad** under the hammock.
- Keep the tarp **separate from and angled over** the hammock so rain runs off and never touches the net.
- Tie high enough that the sagging bottom clears ground vegetation; check overhead for **deadfall branches** and the trunks for **ant columns**.

**If you must sleep on the ground (last resort):** closed-cell pad + fine-mesh netted bivvy + permethrin. Markedly less safe than a hammock here.

## 3. Water

- **Boiling is the single best method** — kills viruses, bacteria, and parasites (including *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*). 1 minute rolling boil.
- **Filter + disinfect is second-best** — a filter with **absolute pore size ≤0.3 micron** removes bacteria/parasites but **not viruses**, so also disinfect or boil.
- **Sourcing (assume everything is contaminated — purify all of it):**
  - Prefer **clear running water** (streams/rivers); **rainwater collected on a clean tarp** is excellent.
  - **Vines** can yield water: cut high, then cut the lower end — **clear fluid is usable; milky/sticky sap = do not drink** (may be toxic).
  - **Seep holes:** dig ~1 m from a muddy stream bank in sandy soil; clearer water seeps in — still purify it.
  - **Transpiration bags:** tie a clear plastic bag over a leafy branch to collect condensation.

## 4. Fire in wet conditions

- **The dry wood is inside the wet wood.** Take **standing dead branches** (not wood off the soaked ground), **split them to the dry core**, and shave **feather sticks**.
- **Wet-proof tinder:** fatwood/resinous pine, papery inner bark, dry termite-mound material, coconut/palm fiber — and **carried** tinder (petroleum-jelly cotton balls, a candle stub, ferro rod + prepared tinder).
- **Build:** raise the fire on a platform of sticks/bark off the wet ground; gather **3× the fuel you think you need**; feed thin-to-thick; shield from wind/rain.
- **Never** use gasoline/alcohol to boost a struggling fire (flash-burn risk); never build under rain-loaded branches or inside a tent.
- Realistically: in saturated conditions fire is slow and labor-intensive — **carry an ignition source and dry tinder**; don't count on friction methods in high humidity.

## 5. Navigation — rescue is unlikely, you'll walk yourself out

- Carry and use a **compass (tied to your body) + topographic map**; GPS is a helpful aid, not a substitute. Mark your route so you can backtrack.
- Move in **short bounds** between identifiable features; use **handrails** — rivers, ridges, coastlines. Following a river **downstream** is the classic exit strategy in lowland jungle.
- **Pace count** — people consistently overestimate distance traveled. Jungle maps can be inaccurate; trails may not match the map.
- **If lost:** stop, don't panic, backtrack to your last known point, **don't travel at night**. Get to a river and follow it downstream — **but note: in Darién rivers can lead deeper into uninhabited terrain**, so verify against local geography before relying on this.

## 6. Food — carry it; foraging is unreliable

- **Food is your lowest priority in the first days** — water, shelter, and signaling come first (a fit adult can go weeks without food, days without water).
- **Foraging is unreliable and risky:** poisonous and edible plants frequently resemble each other; "animals eat it" is not a valid test; **never eat anything you can't positively identify**; avoid mushrooms entirely. Wild food spoils fast in the heat.
- **Carry calorie-dense, non-perishable food** (nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, jerky, peanut butter) — more than you think. Treat any successful forage as a supplement, not the plan.

## 7. Clothing & footwear

- **Long sleeves and long pants, always** — protects skin from bites and cuts; the real wound risk is infection.
- **Waterproof high-ankle boots** with drainage; add **gaiters** (thorn/leech-proof). Rubber/gum boots + leech-proof socks are common in wet terrain.
- **Permethrin-treat** clothing, socks, boots, and net. **Repellent** on exposed skin.
- Hat or head net. A **machete** (the standard jungle tool — see `shelter.md`) and a **walking stick** to probe ahead and part vegetation before your hands do.
- Avoid cotton for anything that must dry or insulate.

## 8. Snakebite first aid (the parts that matter — WHO)

- **Do:** move away from the snake; remove rings/jewelry/tight items from the bitten limb; **keep the person calm and still and immobilize the limb with a splint**; evacuate to medical care as fast as possible; recovery position if vomiting; paracetamol for pain.
- **Do NOT:** tourniquet; cut/suck the wound; ice; electric shock; "black stones"; herbal remedies.
- **Critical distinction:** the **pressure-immobilization bandage (PIB) is NOT for pit-viper bites** (terciopelo/bushmaster/eyelash viper) — it worsens local tissue damage. **PIB is only for neurotoxic bites (coral snake).**
- **Definitive treatment is antivenom** (e.g. Instituto Clodomiro Picado polyvalent covers *Bothrops asper* and *Lachesis*). Priority is evacuation, not field "cures."
